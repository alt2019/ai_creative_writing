<story_development_guides>

<character_knowledge_bubble>
- Each character has their own “knowledge bubble” — that is, the amount of knowledge about what is happening in the plot at a specific point in the narrative, filtered through the prism of subjective perception. It depends only on what the character has personally experienced — heard/saw/did/said/felt, etc. This also includes knowledge about other characters. That is, a character cannot know something that is not contained in their “knowledge bubble.”
- The knowledge bubble includes not only plot events but also the limitations of the character’s basic knowledge (their education, era, culture, profession). A character cannot use concepts, words, or facts that go beyond their social and historical background.
- Each character’s “knowledge bubble” can be gigantic if every little detail is included in it. Therefore, it should be filled only with events important to the plot (filtered through the character’s subjective perception).
- A character can draw conclusions/act only on the basis of their own experience and “knowledge bubble,” adjusted for errors of subjective perception. Errors of perception depend on the character’s current state (mental/physical), their experience, and the external environment (for example, darkness, fog, heat or cold, etc.).
- The author knows the objective (real) events, but the character acts only on the basis of their subjective picture. The task is to show the discrepancy between them when it is important to the plot.
- The “knowledge bubble” is updated continuously throughout the character’s life. Example: a conversation between two characters during which each learned something new.
- A character may reject new information if it contradicts their beliefs, or interpret it in favor of their old worldview. Changing the knowledge bubble is a process stretched out over time and dependent on the individual.
</character_knowledge_bubble>

<plot_causality>
- Plot is a causal chain: every event must follow from earlier events, character choices, or established world rules. Coincidence can complicate the situation, but it should not resolve it.
- Characters drive plot through goals, decisions, and actions. Avoid events that happen only because the author needs them to happen.
- Track two layers: the objective plot (what actually happens) and the subjective plot (what each character believes is happening, based on their knowledge bubble).
- Every major event should have a cause, agent(s), immediate effect, and later consequence.
</plot_causality>

<information_flow>
- Information is a plot resource. Track who knows what, when, and how they learned it.
- A character cannot act on information outside their knowledge bubble. If the plot needs them to know something, first create a plausible path for them to learn it.
- Reveals, secrets, lies, misunderstandings, and discoveries are plot events because they change available choices.
- Dramatic irony (reader/author knows more than character) can raise tension, but character behavior must stay consistent with their subjective knowledge bubble.
</information_flow>

<event_granularity_and_temporal_cohesion>
- Distinguish between plot events (major turning points, milestones, irreversible changes) and micro-events (individual actions, lines of dialogue, beats within an interaction).
- A scene is a continuous unit of time, place, POV, and action. Within a single scene, many micro-events can occur: a punch, a block, a line, a glance, a kiss, a step. These are beats, not separate plot events.
- When the narrative focuses on a high-detail interaction—fight, conversation, training, sex, dance, negotiation—the micro-events are causally and temporally bound. They follow each other within a tight time window, often seconds or minutes.
- Do not distribute beats of one continuous interaction across different days, locations, or unrelated scenes unless the story explicitly requires a time skip or interruption. If a time skip occurs, it must be motivated by plot, character choice, or external event.
- Planning should treat such an interaction as a single scene containing multiple beats. If an outline lists several actions or lines, they belong to the same scene unless there is a clear break in time, place, POV, or focus.
- Each micro-event can update knowledge bubbles, change tactics, escalate tension, or reveal character. But these updates happen within the same scene, not as separate scenes.
- Maintain temporal continuity: actions, reactions, and dialogue should flow in a plausible sequence without unexplained gaps. If a gap is needed, signal it clearly (e.g., “Later that day,” “After a long pause,” “The next morning”).
- Use scene breaks or chapter breaks only for meaningful shifts in time, place, POV, or focus. Do not use them to separate beats that belong together.
- High-detail scenes slow pacing. Use them purposefully for key moments. When detail is high, keep the interaction continuous; when detail is low, summarize or skip.
</event_granularity_and_temporal_cohesion>

<foreshadowing_and_payoff>
- Plant details early that later matter. Payoff must be set up in advance and consistent with established rules.
- Twists should be surprising yet inevitable: rooted in earlier information and knowledge bubbles, not authorial cheating.
- No deus ex machina: solutions must come from established characters, abilities, knowledge, or rules.
</foreshadowing_and_payoff>

</story_development_guides>
